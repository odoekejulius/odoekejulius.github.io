Social media use and student wellbeing.

Prepared by Julius Chibuzo Odo-Eke · Report dated October 30, 2026 · 4,500 records, 16 source fields

This project checks the supplied student dataset, groups daily social media use into broad tiers, and examines how usage relates to wellbeing, stress, sleep, and academic performance. It also checks platform differences, late-night use, age-group patterns, and the usefulness of the provided impact label.

Important data caveat: the report notes unusually smooth relationships—usage alone explains about 72% of mental-health-index variation, while many other effects are close to zero. This pattern is typical of simulated or heavily engineered data. The analysis is a demonstration; validate its magnitudes on real survey data before drawing policy conclusions.

Questions explored

  • How do wellbeing, stress, sleep, and grades vary across daily-use tiers?
  • How large are the reported associations per additional hour of use?
  • Do platform, late-night use, or age group add meaningful differences after accounting for usage?
  • Does the composite impact label add independent evidence, or does it closely track the other outcomes?
  • What data-quality issues and limitations should inform interpretation?

Data quality and interpretation

  • No duplicate rows or student IDs were found in the supplied file. Stress was missing for 46 records and GPA for 85; the delivered analysis retained those gaps and used complete cases for the respective measures.
  • Usage, sleep, stress, and GPA values were checked for consistency and plausible ranges. Documented ceiling effects and unusual values were kept rather than silently removed.
  • The report treats Overall_Impact as a composite label, not an independent outcome: usage alone predicts it with 89.1% accuracy against an 81.8% majority-class baseline.
  • Platform comparisons were not statistically significant after adjustment. The report also says the usage associations were similar for minors and adults; this does not establish that the groups have identical experiences.
  • Adding sleep and stress reduced the usage–GPA association by about 37% in the reported model comparison. This statistical adjustment does not establish mediation; most of the association remained unexplained by those variables.
